What Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Do?

A seismic structural engineer analyzes and designs structures so that they can resist earthquake-related demands under the criteria applicable to a particular project. The work can range from designing the structural system of a new commercial building to evaluating an existing industrial facility for seismic deficiencies.

Seismic Structural Engineering vs. General Structural Engineering

Structural engineering addresses a broad range of loads and performance requirements, including gravity, wind, thermal, and other applicable forces. Seismic structural engineering focuses specifically on earthquake effects and the structural response associated with those effects.

The engineer may evaluate beams, columns, shear walls, moment frames, braced frames, diaphragms, foundations, and structural connections. For some projects, the scope may also include nonstructural components such as mechanical equipment anchorage or MEP seismic support.

Seismic Load Path

One of the central concepts is the seismic load path:

Seismic Hazard → Building Response → Structural System → Connections → Foundations

Earthquake forces must ultimately be transferred through connected structural elements into the foundation system. A discontinuity, weak connection, inadequate member, or poorly coordinated attachment can affect the intended load-transfer mechanism.

Engineering Scope

A seismic structural engineer may prepare seismic calculations, structural analysis, design drawings, connection details, assessment reports, retrofit documents, and construction-support information. Coordination with architects, MEP engineers, contractors, equipment manufacturers, and the Engineer of Record may also be necessary.

For projects involving seismic equipment anchorage or MEP restraints, the structural engineer must understand how forces transfer from the supported component into the structure. This is where structural engineering, seismic bracing, and equipment support become closely connected.

When Does a Building Project Need a Seismic Structural Engineer?

A seismic structural engineer may become involved at several stages of a building's life cycle. New construction, renovation, equipment installation, structural alteration, seismic assessment, and retrofit work can all create a need for specialized seismic engineering.

New Construction

For new commercial or industrial construction, seismic design should be considered from the earliest structural planning stages. The structural system, member sizes, lateral-force-resisting system, diaphragm behavior, connections, and foundation interface should be coordinated with architectural and MEP requirements.

Early engineering coordination can prevent situations where structural members or walls conflict with mechanical equipment, large openings, ductwork, piping, or electrical infrastructure.

Existing Buildings and Renovations

Existing buildings can require seismic evaluation when structural members are altered, new equipment is installed, building use changes, additions are constructed, or significant renovations modify the existing load path.

Older structures may also have incomplete documentation. In these cases, the engineer may need to combine existing drawings with field verification and structural analysis.

Earthquake Damage

Following an earthquake, engineering evaluation may be needed to determine whether observed cracking, deformation, connection damage, foundation movement, or other conditions affect structural performance. Visual inspection is useful for documenting conditions, but engineering analysis may be necessary to determine the significance of observed damage and identify appropriate repairs.

The scope should always be defined around the building, observed conditions, applicable requirements, and the objectives of the owner and authorities having jurisdiction.

How Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Evaluate Earthquake Loads?

Seismic analysis begins with establishing the criteria that govern the project. The engineer then evaluates the building's characteristics and determines how earthquake demand is distributed through the structural system.

Seismic Design Criteria

Project location, site conditions, Seismic Design Category, Risk Category, structural characteristics, and applicable code requirements can influence seismic design. The adopted building code and project-specific structural criteria should be established before calculations are finalized.

Seismic Forces

Seismic demand is not simply a single horizontal force applied to a building. Earthquake response can produce forces and deformations throughout the structure. Engineers evaluate how those demands affect individual floors, structural members, connections, diaphragms, and lateral systems.

Analysis Methods

Depending on the structure and applicable requirements, analysis can involve an equivalent lateral force procedure, modal or response-spectrum analysis, or other appropriate analytical methods. More complex structures may require more sophisticated dynamic analysis.

The selected method depends on structural configuration, height, regularity, seismic characteristics, code requirements, and project objectives.

Structural Response

Engineers may evaluate base shear, story forces, interstory drift, member demand, deformation, overturning effects, and connection forces. The analysis should establish whether the structural system can resist the applicable demands while maintaining the performance required by the project.

The result is more than a calculation of earthquake force. It is an evaluation of how the complete structural system behaves and transfers that demand.

How Is a Building's Seismic Load Path Designed?

A reliable seismic load path connects the point where earthquake forces develop to the foundation through structural components capable of transferring those forces. The design must account for both strength and the behavior of the overall structural system.

Lateral-Force-Resisting Systems

Depending on the building, lateral resistance may be provided by shear walls, moment frames, braced frames, or combinations of these systems. Floor and roof diaphragms distribute seismic forces to the vertical elements of the lateral-force-resisting system.

Collectors and chords can also play important roles in transferring diaphragm forces. Their design must be coordinated with the geometry and continuity of the overall structural system.

Connections and Force Transfer

Connections are critical because structural members cannot transfer seismic forces effectively if their interfaces are inadequate. Bolted and welded connections may require detailed engineering based on the forces generated by the structural analysis.

Connection design can become especially important where existing structures are modified or where new structural components connect to older framing.

Foundation Interface

The seismic load path ultimately reaches the foundation. Lateral forces, overturning effects, and other demands must be transferred through the structural frame and foundation system in accordance with the applicable engineering criteria.

Foundation evaluation may require consideration of reinforced concrete capacity, anchorage, reinforcement, soil conditions, and the relationship between the superstructure and foundation. The exact scope depends on the project and available geotechnical and structural information.

What Is Involved in Seismic Structural Assessment of an Existing Building?

Seismic structural assessment of an existing building requires a different workflow from designing a new structure because the engineer must establish what is actually present before evaluating its capacity.

Existing Drawings and Documentation

The first step can include collecting structural drawings, architectural plans, previous calculations, construction documents, renovation records, material specifications, and available as-built information.

These documents help establish the original structural system, but they should not automatically be treated as proof of current conditions.

Field Verification

Field verification can identify differences between drawings and the constructed building. Engineers may document member dimensions, framing arrangements, connections, foundation conditions, modifications, equipment additions, and other relevant conditions.

For concealed structural components, additional investigation may be required depending on the project's scope.

Structural Capacity

The assessment can consider the capacity of structural members, connections, diaphragms, lateral systems, and foundations under the applicable criteria. Existing material properties may need to be established from available records or appropriate investigation.

Seismic Deficiencies

Potential deficiencies can include inadequate lateral resistance, weak or discontinuous load paths, insufficient connections, insufficient anchorage, excessive deformation, or modifications that changed the original structural behavior.

An assessment does not automatically mean that an entire building requires reconstruction. Engineering may identify localized strengthening or other targeted interventions appropriate to the project's objectives and constraints.

How Does Seismic Retrofit Engineering Strengthen Existing Structures?

Seismic retrofit engineering adapts an existing building to address identified seismic deficiencies while accounting for the realities of an existing structure.

Retrofit Objectives

Retrofit objectives vary according to building use, existing structural conditions, applicable requirements, owner objectives, occupancy, and project scope. A hospital, industrial facility, office building, and historic structure may have very different constraints.

The engineer must first understand the existing load path and identify the specific deficiencies before selecting a strengthening strategy.

Structural Strengthening

Depending on the analysis, retrofit measures may involve additional structural members, shear walls, braced frames, moment-resisting elements, diaphragm improvements, foundation modifications, or localized strengthening.

The selected strategy should address the actual deficiency rather than simply adding material or increasing member size without understanding the structural response.

Connection and Anchorage Improvements

Existing connections may require strengthening through additional plates, bolts, welds, brackets, anchors, or other engineered details. Equipment anchorage can similarly require attachment plates, structural brackets, or mounting assemblies.

Retrofit Constraints

Existing buildings often contain occupied spaces, utilities, architectural finishes, ceilings, MEP systems, and limited access. Construction sequencing can therefore influence the feasibility of a retrofit solution.

BIM and field verification can help identify conflicts before fabrication. Where standard components cannot fit existing conditions, custom steel assemblies may provide a controlled way to implement engineered modifications.

How Does Seismic Engineering Address Nonstructural Components and MEP Systems?

Seismic structural engineering can extend beyond the primary building frame when the project scope includes nonstructural seismic protection. MEP equipment and distribution systems can experience significant earthquake-induced movement even when the primary structure remains functional.

MEP Equipment Anchorage

Mechanical equipment such as air handling units, chillers, pumps, boilers, generators, and rooftop equipment may require seismic anchorage based on applicable project requirements.

The engineering relationship can be represented as:

Equipment → Mounting System → Seismic Restraint → Connection → Anchorage → Structural Member

The equipment support and its structural attachment must be coordinated rather than evaluated as unrelated components.

Seismic Bracing

Suspended HVAC ductwork, piping, electrical conduit, and cable trays may require engineered seismic restraint. MEP trapezes and strut channel systems can provide support while also serving as part of a seismic restraint assembly when properly designed.

Equipment Supports

Mounting frames, equipment bases, attachment plates, brackets, and custom steel assemblies may be required when equipment dimensions or structural conditions do not align with standard support arrangements.

How Do ASCE 7, IBC, and CBC Affect Seismic Structural Engineering?

Seismic structural design in the United States is governed by the applicable adopted building code and referenced standards. The exact requirements depend on the project location, code edition, building type, jurisdiction, and engineering scope.

ASCE 7

ASCE 7 provides widely used provisions for determining seismic design criteria and evaluating structural and nonstructural seismic demands. It addresses factors associated with seismic hazard, structural response, and nonstructural component design.

The applicable edition should be determined from the project's governing code rather than automatically assuming that the latest edition applies.

International Building Code

The IBC establishes a broad framework for structural and building requirements and is adopted in various forms by jurisdictions throughout the United States. The adopted edition and local amendments determine the actual requirements applicable to a specific project.

California Building Code

For California projects, the CBC provides the relevant state-level building-code framework. Seismic structural engineers working on California projects must coordinate the CBC with ASCE 7, local amendments, project specifications, and jurisdictional requirements.

HCAI and OSHPD

Healthcare projects in California may fall under requirements administered by the California Department of Health Care Access and Information, historically associated with OSHPD. Such projects can involve specialized review, documentation, and approval requirements.

The Sigma Source's experience with OSHPD/HCAI-related seismic work is relevant to projects where healthcare requirements intersect with seismic engineering, equipment support, and MEP systems.

Engineer of Record Requirements

The Engineer of Record establishes or coordinates project-specific structural requirements and remains central to determining design responsibility. Outside engineering and fabrication resources should work within the project's defined professional responsibilities and approved design process.

What Structural Systems and Materials Are Evaluated in Seismic Design?

Seismic structural engineering involves both the behavior of the structural system and the properties of the materials and connections used to construct it.

Structural Systems

Engineers may evaluate structural steel frames, reinforced concrete structures, shear walls, moment frames, braced frames, diaphragms, foundations, and structural connections.

Each system responds differently to seismic forces. The design therefore considers not only individual member strength but also system-level behavior, force distribution, deformation, continuity, and connections.

Steel and Metal Components

Structural steel, carbon steel, high-strength steel, alloy steel, stainless steel, steel plate, and structural shapes may be used for primary framing, retrofit components, brackets, equipment supports, and fabricated assemblies.

Material selection depends on structural demand, environmental exposure, fabrication requirements, connection design, and project specifications.

Concrete and Anchorage

Reinforced concrete can form slabs, beams, columns, shear walls, foundations, and other structural elements. Seismic anchorage into concrete may involve anchor bolts, mechanical anchors, adhesive anchors where appropriate, or embedded components.

Concrete strength, reinforcement, edge distance, embedment, spacing, and substrate conditions can influence connection capacity.

Fabrication and Protection

Bolted and welded connections require details appropriate to their intended loads. Fabricated steel components may also require galvanizing, powder coating, or another protective finish when environmental conditions warrant it.

The fabrication process should follow the approved engineering details rather than treating a standard fabricated component as universally suitable for every seismic application.

How Does BIM 3D CAD Support Seismic Structural Engineering?

BIM 3D CAD modeling provides a spatial coordination layer between engineering calculations and physical construction. This becomes particularly valuable when structural members, MEP systems, equipment, and seismic restraints occupy the same limited spaces.

Structural and MEP Coordination

A coordinated model can show beams, columns, slabs, walls, equipment, ductwork, piping, cable trays, conduit, ceilings, and architectural components together. This allows proposed structural attachments and seismic restraints to be reviewed against the actual building environment.

Clash Detection

Typical conflicts include seismic braces crossing ductwork, structural attachments interfering with piping, equipment bases blocking access, and proposed anchors landing in unsuitable structural locations.

Identifying these conditions during design can reduce field modifications and help maintain the intended engineering load path.

Structural Detailing

BIM/CAD can support detailed connection geometry, fabrication drawings, attachment plates, brackets, equipment bases, custom strut channels, and custom steel assemblies.

Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

A practical workflow can be represented as:

Structural Analysis → BIM/CAD Coordination → Engineering Details → Fabrication → Installation → Field Verification

When Are Custom Structural or Seismic Support Assemblies Needed?

Standard components can be effective when their geometry, capacity, connection method, and installation conditions match the project. Custom assemblies become useful when existing conditions or project requirements fall outside those standard configurations.

Nonstandard Geometry

Unusual structural framing, limited ceiling height, irregular equipment layouts, and congested mechanical rooms can make conventional support configurations difficult to install.

A custom bracket or fabricated attachment plate may allow the restraint to connect to a structurally appropriate location without interfering with surrounding systems.

Equipment-Specific Supports

Large or specialized equipment may have unusual mounting points, concentrated loads, or dimensional constraints. Custom mounting frames and structural bases can be designed around the actual equipment geometry.

Custom Strut Channels

Custom strut channels can help accommodate nonstandard dimensions and support configurations for MEP and seismic applications. Their use should still be based on the engineering requirements of the specific assembly.

Retrofit Conditions

Retrofit projects frequently require custom solutions because existing structural members and attachment points are already occupied or difficult to access.

What Information Should You Provide When Requesting Seismic Structural Engineering?

Providing accurate project information at the beginning of an engineering engagement helps establish the appropriate scope and reduces uncertainty during analysis and coordination.

Project Information

Useful information includes the project location, building type, construction stage, existing or new construction status, project scope, occupancy, and applicable jurisdiction.

Structural Information

Available structural drawings, framing plans, foundation drawings, previous calculations, material specifications, renovation documents, and as-built information can help establish the existing structural system.

Seismic Information

Available seismic design criteria, project specifications, geotechnical information, Risk Category, Seismic Design Category, and other applicable structural assumptions should be provided when available.

MEP and Equipment Information

For projects involving equipment anchorage or nonstructural seismic design, provide equipment schedules, weights, dimensions, support locations, manufacturer data, MEP drawings, and proposed attachment points.

BIM and CAD Information

BIM models, CAD files, field measurements, photographs, point-cloud data, and existing-condition surveys can help resolve discrepancies between documentation and actual construction.

A complete information package allows the engineer to evaluate the project as a connected system rather than making isolated assumptions about individual components. Conclusion

A seismic structural engineer provides specialized analysis and design focused on how buildings and their components respond to earthquake-related demands. For new construction, the work can begin with seismic design criteria and continue through structural analysis, lateral-system design, connection detailing, foundation coordination, and construction documentation. For existing buildings, the process can include documentation review, field verification, structural assessment, deficiency identification, and seismic retrofit engineering.

The central engineering principle is the continuity of the seismic load path. Earthquake forces must move through structural members, diaphragms, connections, and foundations in a manner consistent with the applicable design criteria. The same principle applies when the project includes nonstructural components such as HVAC equipment, piping, ductwork, conduit, cable trays, and other MEP systems.

Codes and standards provide the framework, but project-specific engineering determines the actual solution. ASCE 7, the IBC, CBC, and applicable HCAI requirements must be considered in relation to the adopted code edition, jurisdiction, structural system, building characteristics, and project scope. A component described as seismic-rated does not by itself establish that an entire structural or MEP support assembly satisfies project requirements.
