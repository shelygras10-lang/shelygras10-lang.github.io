Seismic Bracing: Engineering Systems, Design Requirements, and MEP Applications

anitaa September 28, 2026

Seismic bracing is an engineered restraint strategy used to limit earthquake-induced movement in mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and other nonstructural building systems. Unlike ordinary supports that primarily carry gravity loads, seismic bracing must address lateral and, where applicable, vertical seismic effects and transfer those forces through a continuous structural load path. That path can extend from a pipe, duct, conduit, cable tray, or mechanical unit through its support and brace assembly, connection, anchor, and finally into the building structure.

For U.S. construction projects, seismic bracing design is closely connected to project-specific seismic criteria, the applicable building code, the component characteristics, and the supporting structure. ASCE/SEI 7 provides important provisions for nonstructural components, including seismic design considerations addressed in Chapter 13, while the adopted edition of the International Building Code (IBC), California Building Code (CBC), and jurisdiction-specific requirements establish the regulatory framework applicable to a project. Healthcare facilities may also require coordination with Health Care Access and Information (HCAI), formerly associated with OSHPD requirements.

Effective seismic bracing therefore involves considerably more than selecting a brace or installing additional hardware. Engineers must consider Seismic Design Category, component weight, support spacing, brace geometry, connection capacity, anchorage, structural capacity, building movement, installation conditions, and coordination with other MEP systems. Equipment that also requires vibration isolation introduces another layer of engineering because seismic restraint must accommodate the intended operational movement of the isolator without unnecessarily compromising vibration performance.

For structural engineers, MEP contractors, architects, general contractors, facility managers, and specification writers, understanding this complete load path is essential. A properly engineered seismic bracing system connects seismic analysis with practical support design, BIM coordination, fabrication, installation, and field verification.

What Is Seismic Bracing and Why Is It Required for Building Systems?

Seismic Bracing vs. Ordinary MEP Supports

Ordinary MEP supports are generally designed to carry gravity loads, maintain equipment or utility positioning, and accommodate normal operating conditions. Seismic bracing serves a different structural function. During an earthquake, building movement can cause suspended or supported components to move laterally relative to the structure. Without an appropriate restraint strategy, that movement can produce impact, displacement, connection failure, or damage to the supported system and adjacent construction.

A seismic brace introduces a controlled load-transfer mechanism. For example, a suspended pipe may be supported vertically by threaded rods and a trapeze. A seismic restraint assembly can then limit lateral movement by connecting the support to an appropriate structural attachment point. The brace itself is only one part of the system. Its capacity must work with the connection, anchor, support assembly, and structure receiving the load.

The Seismic Load Path

A useful way to evaluate seismic bracing is to trace the load path:

Supported Component → Support/Hanger → Seismic Brace → Connection → Anchor → Structural Member

If any link is inadequate, the brace assembly may not provide the intended performance. A brace member with substantial capacity does not compensate for an undersized concrete anchor or a structural attachment that cannot accept the applied demand.

This principle applies to HVAC ductwork, mechanical piping, fire protection piping, electrical conduit, cable trays, suspended equipment, and other nonstructural components. The objective is not simply to prevent movement in one direction but to develop an engineered restraint strategy appropriate to the component and project conditions.

Seismic bracing can also protect facility functionality. Damage to HVAC, electrical distribution, plumbing, fire protection, communications, or critical mechanical equipment can create consequences beyond the immediate component failure. Hospitals, laboratories, data centers, industrial facilities, and other critical environments may therefore require particularly careful coordination of nonstructural seismic protection.

How Do Engineers Determine Seismic Bracing Requirements?

Seismic bracing requirements cannot be established reliably from a universal brace-spacing rule or a generic hardware selection chart. The engineering process begins with the project seismic criteria and then considers the characteristics of the supported component and its connection to the building.

Seismic Design Category and Project Criteria

The project's Seismic Design Category is an important starting point because seismic design provisions applicable to nonstructural components depend on the seismic characteristics of the building and project. Risk Category, component characteristics, location within the building, and applicable design parameters can also affect the evaluation.

ASCE/SEI 7 provides provisions addressing seismic design of nonstructural components. The adopted edition, project specifications, and authority having jurisdiction should always be verified rather than assuming that a particular provision applies identically to every project.

Component Characteristics

Engineers may need information such as component weight, dimensions, support configuration, center of gravity, attachment method, flexibility, and location. A lightweight conduit installation and a large suspended mechanical assembly do not necessarily behave in the same manner, even when they occupy the same ceiling zone.

Equipment anchorage similarly depends on equipment mass, support geometry, base configuration, anchor locations, and the capacity of the supporting slab or framing.

Seismic Design Force and Load Transfer

Once the applicable design criteria are established, the engineering evaluation determines the seismic demand for the relevant component. That demand then has to be transferred through the support and restraint system into the structure.

The analysis should therefore continue beyond the brace member. Brace angle, member length, attachment geometry, connection type, anchor capacity, and supporting structural member capacity all influence the final load path.

For a project involving numerous MEP systems, seismic bracing calculations and coordinated drawings can help establish where restraints are required, how they are configured, and how they interact with the architectural and structural design. This project-specific approach is particularly important when standard components are being adapted to unusual field conditions.

What Types of Seismic Bracing Systems Are Used for MEP Applications?

MEP seismic bracing systems vary according to the supported component, available structural attachment points, required restraint directions, and project-specific seismic demand. The goal is to create an efficient load path without interfering with normal system operation, maintenance, or required movement.

Seismic Trapeze and Strut Systems

A seismic trapeze can support multiple pipes, conduits, or other services while incorporating lateral restraint into the assembly. Strut channel systems are frequently useful because they can be configured into support frames, brackets, and brace assemblies.

Where standard dimensions do not align with actual field requirements, custom strut channels or fabricated steel components can provide a more controlled interface between the MEP system and structural attachment.

Rigid Brace Assemblies

Rigid seismic brace assemblies can use steel brace members, brackets, strut components, threaded hardware, and structural attachments. Their geometry must be evaluated because brace angle and connection location influence the forces delivered to both ends of the assembly.

Cable-Based Restraint

Cable seismic bracing may be appropriate for certain installations when the system, components, and applicable approvals support its use. The engineering decision should consider the required restraint directions, installation geometry, connection details, and project requirements rather than treating cable and rigid systems as interchangeable.

Directional Restraint

Transverse and longitudinal restraints address movement in different directions. Some MEP arrangements require restraint in multiple directions, and the appropriate configuration depends on the component geometry and seismic design requirements.

The selection process should begin with the supported system and required load path rather than with a particular hardware product. This helps ensure that seismic restraint becomes part of the overall MEP support design instead of an afterthought added during construction.

How Is HVAC Ductwork Designed for Seismic Bracing?

HVAC ductwork presents a distinct seismic bracing challenge because ducts are relatively large distributed components supported throughout a building while also connecting to equipment and architectural openings. Their seismic restraint must work with the duct support system, structural attachment, and movement requirements at equipment interfaces.

Duct Support and Seismic Restraint

A conventional duct hanger supports the gravity weight of the duct and its contents. Seismic restraint addresses earthquake-induced movement. These functions can be incorporated into a coordinated support arrangement, but they should not be assumed to be identical.

Brace locations, support spacing, duct geometry, connection details, and structural attachment conditions all affect the design. Large ducts may also require reinforcement or specialized support arrangements so that the restraint forces can be transferred effectively.

Transverse and Longitudinal Restraint

Transverse restraint limits movement across the duct's primary direction, while longitudinal restraint addresses movement along that direction. Both directions can be relevant depending on the installation and project criteria.

Flexible Connections

Ducts connected to air handling units, fans, or other vibrating mechanical equipment may require flexible connections. A rigid connection can provide an unintended path for operational vibration and can also interfere with movement that the equipment support system is intended to accommodate.

The same principle applies when HVAC equipment is installed on rooftops. Engineers must consider the roof structure, equipment support, wind exposure, seismic movement, penetrations, and nearby architectural elements. BIM coordination can be particularly valuable in dense mechanical spaces where braces, ducts, piping, electrical systems, and structural members compete for limited installation space.

For contractors, early coordination can reduce field modifications and help maintain the engineered brace geometry shown in approved drawings.

How Does Seismic Bracing Protect Piping, Conduit, and Cable Tray Systems?

Seismic restraint for piping and electrical systems requires consideration of the component's support arrangement, movement characteristics, restraint direction, connection capacity, and structural attachment. Applying the same configuration to every MEP system can overlook important differences in behavior and installation.

Seismic Pipe Bracing

Mechanical piping, plumbing, fire protection piping, and industrial process piping can require seismic restraint appropriate to their configuration and project criteria. Pipe support spacing establishes the basic gravity support arrangement, while seismic restraints provide additional control of earthquake-induced movement.

Transverse and longitudinal restraints should be evaluated based on the direction of potential movement and the arrangement of supports. Flexible piping connections may also be necessary at equipment interfaces or other locations where relative movement must be accommodated.

Electrical Conduit

Electrical conduit can experience lateral movement during seismic events. Properly configured support and restraint assemblies connect the conduit system to the structure while maintaining the required installation geometry.

Cable Trays

Cable tray systems present another important consideration because they can contain substantial distributed loads and form part of essential electrical or communications infrastructure. Seismic bracing must coordinate tray support, restraint direction, connection hardware, and structural attachment.

The same load-path principle applies throughout:

MEP Component → Support → Restraint → Connection → Anchorage → Structure

Seismic expansion joints and flexible connectors can become important where building movement or system movement must be accommodated. Restraint should not inadvertently lock a system against movement that has been intentionally provided for elsewhere in the design.

How Are Seismic Restraints for Mechanical Equipment Designed?

Mechanical equipment presents a concentrated seismic load rather than a distributed MEP load. Chillers, pumps, generators, cooling equipment, boilers, air handling units, and rooftop mechanical units may impose substantial forces on their supports and anchors.

Equipment Mass and Anchorage

Equipment mass is a fundamental input to seismic evaluation, but it is not the only consideration. Equipment geometry, center of gravity, support points, base configuration, mounting height, and structural attachment locations can affect the load path.

Equipment anchorage may use anchor bolts, concrete anchors, structural steel connections, mounting plates, brackets, or fabricated frames. The substrate matters: an anchor installed into a concrete slab must be evaluated differently from a connection made directly to structural steel.

Equipment Mounting Frames

Custom structural frames can distribute equipment loads and provide defined attachment locations when the manufacturer's base does not align with available structural supports. Such frames may be fabricated from structural steel, carbon steel, stainless steel, or aluminum depending on the application and environmental conditions.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint solve different problems. Vibration isolation reduces the transmission of operational dynamic forces between equipment and its supporting structure. Seismic restraint limits earthquake-induced movement and transfers seismic demand into the structure.

Some equipment requires both functions. In those situations, restrained vibration isolators, seismic snubbers, anchors, or other coordinated restraint arrangements may be considered. The restraint system must allow the intended operational behavior of the isolation system while providing the required seismic protection.

For rooftop equipment, engineers must additionally coordinate the roof structure, support frames, wind effects, weather exposure, access, and seismic attachment. A properly engineered equipment restraint therefore considers the equipment and the building as one interacting system.

How Do Seismic Bracing Components Transfer Earthquake Forces Into the Structure?

A seismic bracing assembly should be evaluated as a complete load-transfer system. The brace member is only the visible portion of a much larger structural mechanism.

Brace Geometry

Brace angle, member length, orientation, and attachment location influence the forces developed within the assembly. Changing the angle or moving the structural attachment point can change the force delivered to the brace and its connections.

This is why field relocation of an engineered brace can be more significant than it appears. A seemingly minor change can alter the geometry and therefore the load path.

Connection Capacity

Connections require independent consideration. The capacity of the brace does not automatically establish the capacity of its attachment. Bolted, welded, clamped, or threaded connections have different design characteristics and must be appropriate for the actual configuration.

Anchors and Structural Attachments

Concrete anchors must be compatible with the concrete substrate, edge distances, embedment, spacing, installation conditions, and applicable design requirements. Steel attachments must likewise account for the capacity of the connected structural member and the connection itself.

Complete Load Path

The final evaluation follows the force from the MEP component through the support, brace, connection, anchor, and structural member. The supporting slab, beam, joist, or frame must have adequate capacity to receive the applied demand.

This is a critical distinction in seismic bracing engineering: brace-member capacity alone does not establish system adequacy.

What Materials and Components Are Used in Seismic Bracing Systems?

Steel is common in seismic bracing because it can provide the strength, stiffness, and fabrication flexibility required for brace members, brackets, strut systems, trapezes, and structural attachments. Carbon steel and structural steel are widely applicable in conventional building environments, while stainless steel may be selected where corrosion resistance or environmental exposure warrants it.

Galvanized steel and zinc-protected components can be useful for appropriate outdoor or corrosive environments. Powder coating can provide an additional protective finish when compatible with the application. Material selection should be based on service conditions rather than appearance alone.

Anchors and Fasteners

Concrete anchors may be mechanical or adhesive systems when permitted by the project and appropriate for the substrate and loading condition. Anchor bolts, threaded rods, brackets, beam attachments, and steel-to-steel connections all form potential links in the load path.

Custom Fabricated Components

Projects with congested MEP spaces or unusual structural conditions may require custom plates, brackets, frames, or strut configurations. Laser cutting and plasma cutting can produce precise steel components, while forming, stamping, machining, and welding can support more complex assemblies.

Environmental Durability

A seismic restraint system may remain installed for decades, so environmental exposure should be considered during design. Outdoor rooftop systems can encounter moisture and temperature cycling, while industrial installations may involve chemicals, contamination, or elevated temperatures. Marine environments can impose substantially different corrosion requirements.

The appropriate material and coating strategy therefore supports not only initial strength but also long-term durability and maintainability.

How Should Seismic Bracing Be Coordinated With Structural and MEP Design?

Seismic bracing is most effective when it is incorporated during design coordination rather than added after the MEP layout has been finalized. A brace needs a suitable attachment point, adequate clearance, and a compatible load path. Those conditions frequently depend on decisions made by structural, architectural, and MEP teams.

Structural Coordination

Brace attachments may connect to concrete slabs, beams, joists, structural steel, or specifically designed support members. The receiving structure must be capable of accepting the applicable forces, and the attachment location must be compatible with the structural design.

MEP Coordination

Mechanical rooms and ceiling spaces can contain ducts, pipes, conduit, cable trays, lighting, fire protection, and structural framing within a limited volume. A theoretically suitable brace may become impractical if another system occupies the required space.

BIM 3D CAD modeling can help identify these conflicts before fabrication. Three-dimensional coordination can show brace geometry, anchor locations, equipment clearances, maintenance zones, structural members, and utility interfaces. It can also support fabrication drawings when custom assemblies are required.

Seismic Gaps and Movement

Building joints, equipment interfaces, and differential movement conditions require special attention. A restraint should not block an intentional seismic gap or create an unintended rigid connection between systems that must move independently.

Construction Sequencing

Successful installation also depends on shop drawings, approved details, field dimensions, material availability, and construction sequencing. When field conditions differ from design drawings, the appropriate engineering review should occur before modifying the restraint assembly.

This integration of engineering, BIM, fabrication, and construction management helps preserve the intended load path from design through installation.

What Are the Common Seismic Bracing Requirements for Hospitals and Critical Facilities?

Healthcare and critical facilities place additional importance on nonstructural seismic performance because mechanical and electrical systems can be essential to continued building operation. Hospitals may depend on HVAC, emergency power, medical support infrastructure, plumbing, fire protection, communications, and other MEP systems under demanding conditions.

California healthcare projects can involve HCAI requirements and project-specific approval procedures in addition to the applicable building-code and seismic-design framework. The exact requirements depend on the project, facility type, adopted regulations, and authority having jurisdiction.

Critical MEP Systems

A hospital mechanical room may contain chillers, pumps, air handling systems, piping, electrical infrastructure, and equipment supports within a highly coordinated space. Seismic bracing must address the individual systems while preserving access, maintenance requirements, and required movement.

Equipment anchorage is similarly important. A generator or other critical unit must have a defined path for transferring seismic forces into the supporting structure. The equipment base, anchor, structural member, and connection must work together.

Documentation and Approval

Healthcare projects may require calculations, drawings, product data, engineering documentation, installation information, and review by the appropriate parties. HCAI/OSHPD-related work should not be approached as a generic commercial installation because project-specific requirements can affect component selection, detailing, documentation, and approval.

For engineers and contractors, early identification of healthcare seismic requirements can prevent redesign during submittal review or construction. For facility owners, coordinated seismic restraint can form part of a broader effort to maintain the physical integrity and functionality of critical building systems.

When Are Custom Seismic Bracing Systems Required?

Standard seismic bracing components can address many conventional installations, but projects often contain conditions that require a more engineered solution. Customization becomes relevant when structural attachment points, equipment geometry, MEP congestion, loading, or environmental conditions do not align with standard assemblies.

Nonstandard Structural Conditions

A brace may encounter a beam, architectural opening, duct, pipe rack, or other obstruction at the ideal attachment location. Rather than relocating the entire MEP system, a custom bracket or structural attachment plate may provide a more appropriate load path.

Custom Strut Channels and Steel Assemblies

Large trapezes, unusual pipe arrangements, equipment frames, and congested mechanical rooms can benefit from custom strut configurations. Fabricated steel plates, brackets, frames, and welded assemblies can be designed around specific dimensions and attachment requirements.

The fabrication process may include laser or plasma cutting, forming, machining, welding, galvanizing, or powder coating depending on the material and service environment.

Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

A coordinated project can follow a sequence such as:

Seismic Calculations → Brace Design → Structural Coordination → BIM/CAD → Fabrication → Coating → Installation → Field Verification

This workflow connects analytical requirements with the physical assembly that will actually be installed.

How Are Seismic Bracing Systems Evaluated for Compliance?

Compliance is not established simply because a product is labeled “seismic.” The applicable project requirements must be identified and then applied to the actual component, support, connection, anchorage, and structure.

ASCE/SEI 7 provides seismic design provisions relevant to nonstructural components, while the adopted IBC establishes building-code requirements in jurisdictions using that code framework. In California, the CBC provides the applicable state building-code framework, subject to the edition and amendments adopted by the jurisdiction.

Healthcare facilities may involve HCAI requirements in addition to these broader standards. The project Engineer of Record, specifications, authority having jurisdiction, and approved documents should determine which requirements apply.

Documentation

Depending on the project, documentation may include seismic calculations, product data, load ratings, anchor information, material specifications, shop drawings, connection details, installation instructions, and engineering approvals.

The documentation should support the actual installed configuration. If a brace assembly shown in a calculation is changed substantially in the field, the original engineering assumptions may no longer apply.

Field Verification

Field verification helps confirm that brace locations, support spacing, structural attachments, anchors, clearances, and components correspond with the approved design. This becomes particularly important where MEP coordination is complex or where installation conditions differ from the original drawings.

The compliance process is therefore best understood as a chain:

Applicable Criteria → Engineering Demand → Component Design → Connection Design → Anchorage → Structural Verification → Approved Documentation → Installation → Field Verification

That chain provides a much stronger basis for seismic bracing performance than relying on hardware selection alone.

How Should Seismic Bracing Be Installed and Inspected?

Installation is where the engineered seismic load path becomes a physical system. Even a properly calculated brace can perform differently if its geometry, connection, anchor, or attachment location changes during construction.

Brace angle and orientation should correspond to the engineered configuration. Support spacing and restraint locations should also be verified against the approved drawings. Anchors require correct installation in the specified substrate, including the required location and installation procedure.

Avoiding Unapproved Field Modifications

Cutting a brace member, drilling a new connection hole, relocating an anchor, changing a bracket, or moving a restraint can change its structural behavior. These modifications should receive appropriate engineering review rather than being treated as ordinary field adjustments.

Inspection and Verification

Inspection can address visible brace geometry, connection hardware, anchor installation, support configuration, clearances, and required components. Documentation of field conditions can also help resolve discrepancies between design documents and installed systems.

Coordination with other trades is equally important. A completed duct, pipe, conduit, or cable tray installation should not unintentionally block the intended movement of another component or interfere with a seismic restraint.

For contractors and project managers, early review of shop drawings and field conditions is often more efficient than correcting seismic bracing after surrounding construction has been completed. For facility managers, inspection and documentation provide useful records for future maintenance, renovations, and system modifications.

Conclusion: Designing Seismic Bracing as a Complete Load-Path System

Seismic bracing is fundamentally an engineering problem involving movement, force transfer, connections, anchorage, and structural capacity. The most reliable approach begins with project-specific seismic criteria and follows the resulting demand through the supported component, support assembly, brace, connection, anchor, and supporting structure.

For MEP systems, that process applies differently to HVAC ducts, mechanical and fire protection piping, electrical conduit, cable trays, and equipment. Transverse and longitudinal restraint, trapeze configurations, brace geometry, support spacing, structural attachment, and flexible connections must be considered in relation to the actual system. Mechanical equipment introduces additional considerations involving mass, center of gravity, equipment bases, anchors, and structural support frames.

The distinction between seismic bracing and vibration isolation is equally important. Vibration isolation addresses operational dynamic forces, while seismic restraint addresses earthquake-induced movement and load transfer. Equipment may require both, but the two systems should be coordinated so that seismic restraints do not unintentionally defeat the intended isolation performance.

Successful projects also depend on structural and MEP coordination. BIM 3D CAD can identify clashes and attachment constraints before fabrication, while custom strut channels, steel brackets, mounting frames, and other fabricated assemblies can address nonstandard conditions. In healthcare and critical facilities, HCAI requirements and project-specific approval procedures may add further documentation and coordination requirements.

The Sigma Source approaches seismic protection through this connection between engineering and fabrication. Seismic calculations, structural engineering, BIM coordination, custom metal fabrication, and project execution can be integrated to develop restraint assemblies around actual project conditions. The result is not simply a stronger bracket; it is a coordinated load path designed for the component, building structure, installation environment, and applicable requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions About Seismic Bracing

What is seismic bracing?

Seismic bracing is an engineered restraint system that limits earthquake-induced movement of nonstructural components and transfers applicable seismic forces into the supporting structure. It can be used for MEP systems such as ducts, pipes, conduit, cable trays, and mechanical equipment. A complete system includes more than the brace itself: support members, brace connections, anchors, and the supporting structural element all participate in the load path.

When is seismic bracing required for MEP systems?

Requirements depend on the project's seismic design criteria, applicable building code, Seismic Design Category, component characteristics, and jurisdictional requirements. ASCE/SEI 7 contains provisions for seismic design of nonstructural components, while the adopted IBC or CBC establishes the applicable building-code framework. The actual project documents and adopted code edition should be reviewed rather than assuming that every MEP installation requires the same restraint configuration.

What is the difference between seismic bracing and regular MEP support?

Regular MEP supports primarily carry gravity loads and maintain system positioning during normal conditions. Seismic bracing is designed to address earthquake-induced movement and applicable seismic forces. A pipe, for example, can be adequately supported vertically but still require lateral seismic restraint. The two functions can be incorporated into one coordinated support assembly, but they have different engineering purposes.

How does ASCE 7 affect seismic bracing design?

ASCE/SEI 7 provides seismic design provisions that can apply to nonstructural components, including considerations addressed in Chapter 13. The applicable requirements depend on project conditions such as seismic design parameters, component characteristics, and the adopted code framework. ASCE 7 should therefore be treated as part of a project-specific engineering process rather than as a standalone universal brace-selection table.

What types of MEP systems require seismic bracing?

Depending on project requirements, seismic restraint can apply to HVAC ductwork, mechanical piping, plumbing, fire protection systems, electrical conduit, cable trays, telecommunications infrastructure, and mechanical equipment. Each system requires its own consideration of weight, support spacing, geometry, movement, connections, and structural attachment. A restraint arrangement suitable for one MEP system should not automatically be transferred to another without engineering evaluation.

How are HVAC ducts and piping restrained for earthquakes?

HVAC ducts and piping can use transverse and longitudinal restraints configured to limit movement in the required directions. The restraint connects the supported system to an appropriate structural attachment through a brace assembly. Engineers consider support spacing, brace geometry, connection capacity, anchorage, and structural capacity. Flexible connections may also be required at equipment interfaces or locations where differential movement must be accommodated.

Do mechanical equipment and rooftop HVAC units require seismic anchorage?

Whether anchorage is required and how it is designed depends on the equipment, project seismic criteria, applicable code provisions, and installation conditions. Heavy equipment such as chillers, generators, pumps, and rooftop HVAC units can produce significant seismic demands. Evaluation may include equipment mass, center of gravity, base configuration, anchor locations, roof or floor structure, structural connections, and wind exposure. Equipment mounted on vibration isolators may require a coordinated restrained isolation strategy.

What is the difference between transverse and longitudinal seismic bracing?

Transverse restraint limits movement across the primary direction of a supported system, while longitudinal restraint limits movement along that direction. Both can be relevant to piping, ductwork, cable trays, and other linear MEP systems. The appropriate configuration depends on system geometry, support arrangement, seismic demand, and applicable project requirements. Directional restraint should be evaluated as part of the complete load path.

How are seismic bracing systems attached to concrete and structural steel?

Concrete attachments may use approved mechanical or adhesive anchor systems when appropriate for the project and substrate. Structural steel attachments can use bolted, welded, clamped, or other engineered connection methods. The connection and supporting substrate must be evaluated along with the brace member. Anchor capacity, edge distance, embedment, steel-member capacity, connection geometry, and installation conditions can all affect the final system.

Can seismic bracing be combined with vibration isolation systems?

Yes, equipment can require both vibration isolation and seismic restraint, but the two functions must be coordinated. Vibration isolation is intended to reduce operational vibration and dynamic force transmission, while seismic restraint limits earthquake-induced movement and transfers seismic loads. Restrained isolators and other coordinated restraint arrangements can be used where appropriate. The restraint must provide the required seismic control without unnecessarily preventing the movement needed for effective vibration isolation.

When should custom seismic bracing or custom strut channels be used?

Custom systems become useful when standard components cannot accommodate the project's structural attachment locations, MEP geometry, loading, clearances, or environmental conditions. Examples include congested mechanical rooms, large trapeze assemblies, unusual equipment bases, irregular structural framing, and nonstandard attachment points. Custom steel plates, brackets, frames, and strut assemblies can be engineered and fabricated around the actual load path.

What documentation is required for seismic bracing on healthcare projects?

Healthcare requirements vary according to the facility, jurisdiction, adopted regulations, project specifications, and applicable HCAI requirements. Documentation may include seismic calculations, drawings, product data, anchor information, connection details, shop drawings, installation instructions, and engineering approvals. The key objective is to demonstrate that the installed restraint system corresponds to the applicable design criteria and approved engineering. Field verification is especially important when construction conditions differ from the original design.
