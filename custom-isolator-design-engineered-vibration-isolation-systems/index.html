Custom Isolator Design: Engineered Vibration Isolation Systems

anitaa September 28, 2026

Vibration isolation is rarely a matter of choosing a mount based on equipment weight alone. In demanding commercial, industrial, healthcare, marine, and infrastructure applications, the equipment, operating conditions, supporting structure, and required vibration performance must be considered as one connected engineering system. This is where custom isolator design becomes valuable.

A standard vibration mount may have an appropriate nominal load capacity but still be unsuitable because its stiffness, dimensions, mounting pattern, operating frequency range, environmental resistance, or movement characteristics do not match the application. A custom solution instead begins with the actual equipment requirements and develops the isolator around those conditions.

The engineering process can involve equipment weight and center of gravity, load distribution, operating speed, excitation frequency, static deflection, natural frequency, dynamic stiffness, damping, transmissibility, lateral loads, shock conditions, and available mounting space. Material selection also matters. Rubber compounds, spring steel, stainless steel, carbon steel, wire rope, and other materials behave differently under temperature, corrosion, fatigue, and cyclic loading.

Custom isolation may also need to interface with equipment bases, inertia bases, mounting frames, structural steel, concrete supports, flexible connections, and seismic restraints. For building applications, seismic requirements may introduce additional considerations under ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, or project-specific criteria, while healthcare facilities may have additional HCAI requirements.

For engineers, contractors, facility managers, and procurement teams, the objective is not simply to obtain a physically compatible mount. It is to develop an isolation system whose mechanical characteristics are appropriate for the equipment and whose physical configuration can be integrated into the project.

What Is Custom Isolator Design and When Is It Needed?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing a vibration isolation component or assembly around the actual requirements of a particular piece of equipment. Rather than adapting the equipment to a catalog product, the isolation system is developed around the equipment's loading, excitation, geometry, environmental conditions, and performance objectives.

Standard vibration isolators remain appropriate for many applications. However, specialized equipment can create requirements that fall outside typical catalog configurations. An industrial machine may have an unusual bolt pattern or highly concentrated loads. A generator may produce significant dynamic forces while requiring both vibration isolation and seismic restraint. A marine engine may operate in a corrosive environment and experience continuous vibration and shock. An HVAC unit may have limited space beneath the equipment and require a custom mounting frame.

Custom Isolators vs. Standard Vibration Mounts

The difference is fundamentally an engineering issue rather than simply a dimensional one. A standard mount may have the correct nominal capacity but an unsuitable stiffness or deflection characteristic. Conversely, a mount with adequate stiffness may not fit the required footprint or provide the necessary movement control.

Custom design can address:

  • Unequal loads between mounting points

  • Unusual equipment footprints

  • Nonstandard bolt patterns

  • Low-frequency excitation

  • Variable operating speeds

  • High cyclic loading

  • Shock or transient loads

  • Restricted installation space

  • Corrosive or marine environments

  • Elevated temperatures

  • Special structural attachment requirements

Applications can include pumps, fans, compressors, chillers, air handling units, generators, motors, machine tools, production machinery, laboratory equipment, medical equipment, and marine engines.

The appropriate solution may be a custom spring isolator, wire rope isolator, rubber/metal mount, captive isolation assembly, or a more comprehensive equipment mounting system. In many projects, the isolator itself is only one part of the design. Custom brackets, mounting plates, rails, inertia bases, or structural frames may be required to establish a reliable interface between the equipment and the isolation system.

How Are Equipment Loads Determined for Custom Isolator Design?

The starting point for a custom vibration isolator is an accurate understanding of the equipment loads. Total equipment weight provides only the first level of information. Engineers also need to understand how that weight is distributed among the individual mounting points and how operating conditions modify the forces carried by each isolator.

Equipment Weight and Load Distribution

If a piece of equipment is supported by four isolators, it cannot automatically be assumed that each isolator carries exactly one-quarter of the total weight. The center of gravity, support geometry, equipment configuration, and stiffness distribution can produce different reactions at each mounting location.

This becomes especially important when equipment contains heavy motors, transformers, compressors, rotating assemblies, or other concentrated components. Load distribution should therefore be considered when determining the required capacity and stiffness of each individual mount.

Center of Gravity

The center of gravity influences both static loading and equipment stability. If the center of gravity is substantially above the support plane, lateral movement and overturning behavior can become more important. The position of the isolators relative to the equipment's center of gravity can affect rotational response as well.

Static and Dynamic Loads

Static load represents the equipment's supported weight. Dynamic loading can result from rotating imbalance, reciprocating mechanisms, torque reactions, startup and shutdown events, impact, or other operating conditions.

A custom isolator design therefore needs to distinguish between the equipment's static condition and its dynamic operating condition. Depending on the application, engineers may need to evaluate multiple load cases rather than designing around a single nominal weight.

Equipment Load Data

Useful inputs can include manufacturer data, equipment drawings, mounting dimensions, operating speeds, center-of-gravity information, vibration measurements, and known dynamic forces. When these inputs are incomplete, field measurements and additional engineering investigation may be necessary before finalizing the isolation system.

How Does Operating Frequency Affect Custom Vibration Isolator Design?

Operating frequency is one of the most important variables in vibration isolation. A mount that supports the equipment mechanically may still perform poorly as an isolator if its dynamic characteristics are inappropriate for the frequency generated by the equipment.

Equipment Operating Speed

Rotating machinery commonly produces excitation associated with operating speed. For example, an engine or motor operating at a specified RPM generates a corresponding fundamental rotational frequency, while imbalance and mechanical characteristics can introduce additional harmonics.

Reciprocating equipment can produce several excitation components, making frequency analysis more complex. Variable-speed machinery creates another challenge because the operating frequency may change during normal operation.

Natural Frequency

Every isolation system has a characteristic natural frequency influenced by supported mass and effective stiffness. For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system, natural frequency is related to the ratio of stiffness to mass. This relationship is central to understanding whether an isolator can provide meaningful vibration isolation.

Isolation performance generally depends on the relationship between excitation frequency and the isolation system's natural frequency. When excitation occurs near resonance, vibration can become amplified rather than effectively isolated.

Resonance

A poorly selected isolator can place the system near a resonant condition during startup, shutdown, or normal operation. Custom isolator design can therefore consider the complete operating range rather than only the steady-state RPM.

Multiple Excitation Frequencies

Industrial machinery may have several excitation sources simultaneously. Engineers may evaluate the fundamental operating frequency, harmonics, gear-related frequencies, blade-pass frequencies, reciprocating forces, or other known sources.

This is one reason custom vibration isolation engineering should begin with the equipment's actual dynamic behavior. The objective is not simply to choose a softer or harder mount, but to establish a suitable relationship between equipment excitation and isolation-system response.

How Are Static Deflection, Stiffness, and Transmissibility Evaluated?

Static deflection, stiffness, damping, and transmissibility are closely connected in vibration isolation design. Treating any one of these parameters independently can produce an incomplete understanding of system behavior.

Static Deflection

Static deflection describes how much an isolator deflects under the supported equipment load. For spring isolation systems, static deflection is closely related to spring stiffness and supported mass and is an important design parameter when establishing the system's natural frequency.

Greater flexibility can support lower natural frequencies, but increased movement may also create clearance, stability, or restraint challenges. The appropriate deflection is therefore determined by the application rather than by a universal target.

Dynamic Stiffness

Dynamic stiffness describes how an isolator responds under dynamic loading. This can be particularly important for elastomeric materials, whose behavior may depend on frequency, temperature, preload, and material formulation.

The difference between static and dynamic properties means that a design based only on a static load rating may not adequately represent actual operating behavior.

Damping

Damping affects how energy is dissipated within the isolation system. It influences resonance amplitude, transient response, and the shape of the transmissibility response. Higher damping can reduce resonant amplification, although damping characteristics must be evaluated alongside isolation requirements.

Transmissibility

Transmissibility describes the relationship between the excitation and the vibration transmitted through the isolation system. It is affected by frequency ratio, damping, stiffness, and system configuration.

Consequently, claims such as a universal percentage of vibration reduction are rarely meaningful without specifying the frequency range, equipment, mount characteristics, loading, and installation conditions.

A technically sound custom vibration isolator design evaluates these variables together to establish an isolation system appropriate for the actual application.

What Types of Custom Vibration Isolators Can Be Designed?

Different equipment and operating environments call for different isolation technologies. Custom design allows the geometry and configuration to be adapted while preserving the fundamental behavior required by the application.

Custom Spring Isolators

Spring isolators can provide substantial vertical flexibility and are commonly considered for mechanical equipment such as HVAC units, pumps, fans, compressors, and other machinery. Depending on the application, configurations can include open, restrained, or captive springs.

The spring characteristics need to correspond to the supported load and desired dynamic response. Physical dimensions, mounting hardware, travel limits, and restraint requirements also influence the final design.

Custom Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed wire rope elements to create a compliant support system. They can provide multidirectional flexibility and may be useful in applications involving vibration, shock, impact, or demanding environmental conditions.

Stainless steel wire rope configurations can also be considered where corrosion resistance is important, although material selection remains application-specific.

Custom Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber and elastomeric systems can be designed using natural rubber, neoprene, synthetic rubber, laminated elastomers, or rubber/metal assemblies. Their properties can be tailored through material formulation and geometry.

These systems can be useful where compact dimensions, specific stiffness characteristics, or integrated mounting configurations are required.

Captive and Restrained Systems

Some equipment requires controlled movement in addition to vibration isolation. Captive or restrained isolation systems can incorporate mechanical features that limit excessive displacement while allowing the isolator to perform its intended vibration-control function.

The restraint must be designed as part of the complete system. Adding a rigid restraint without considering its interaction with the isolation system can unintentionally create a direct vibration path.

How Does Custom Isolator Design Address Mounting Geometry?

Mechanical compatibility is a fundamental part of successful vibration isolation. Even an appropriately engineered isolator is of limited value if it cannot be securely and accurately connected to the equipment and supporting structure.

Equipment Footprint and Mounting Points

Engineers may need to work around unusual equipment dimensions, existing bolt holes, limited underside clearance, or irregular support surfaces. The position of each isolator can affect load distribution, stability, and rotational behavior.

Custom mounting plates, brackets, rails, and attachment components can provide the necessary interface without requiring major modifications to the equipment.

Uneven Loading

Individual mounts may experience different loads because of equipment geometry or center-of-gravity location. Custom designs can accommodate these variations by assigning appropriate capacity or stiffness to different support locations.

Equipment Stability

A vibration isolation system must support the equipment while controlling unwanted movement. High or offset centers of gravity can increase the importance of lateral stiffness and rotational response.

The design may therefore consider vertical, horizontal, axial, lateral, and torsional behavior rather than treating the equipment as a simple vertically loaded mass.

Equipment Bases and Inertia Bases

In some applications, an inertia base or equipment mounting frame can help integrate the equipment with the isolation system. The fabricated assembly may incorporate steel plates, structural members, brackets, anchor points, and isolator attachment locations.

This approach is particularly useful when the equipment itself lacks suitable mounting geometry or when a larger support interface is required.

How Are Materials Selected for Custom Vibration Isolators?

Material selection is part of the engineering design because the material determines more than basic strength. Temperature, corrosion, fatigue, stiffness, environmental exposure, and service life can all influence material suitability.

Elastomer Selection

Natural rubber, neoprene, synthetic rubber, and other elastomeric compounds can have different mechanical and environmental characteristics. The appropriate compound depends on factors such as required stiffness, operating temperature, moisture, chemicals, ozone exposure, cyclic loading, and expected service life.

For this reason, a rubber isolator should not be specified solely by the generic term "rubber."

Spring and Metal Components

Spring steel is commonly associated with helical spring elements, while carbon steel, stainless steel, alloy steel, structural steel, and aluminum may be appropriate for housings, brackets, plates, frames, and other structural components depending on the application.

Stainless steel can be particularly relevant in corrosive or marine environments, but it should be selected based on the complete mechanical and environmental requirements.

Corrosion and Environmental Exposure

Industrial facilities, outdoor installations, wastewater environments, marine vessels, and coastal projects can expose components to moisture, salt, chemicals, and contaminants. Corrosion resistance should therefore be considered at the material, fastener, weld, and coating levels.

Protective Finishes

Galvanizing, powder coating, and other protective finishes can extend the service life of suitable components. However, coatings must be compatible with operating temperature and exposure conditions. A finish appropriate for a structural support may not be appropriate for a component exposed directly to elevated exhaust or engine temperatures.

How Does Custom Isolator Design Work With Seismic Restraint?

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint address different engineering problems, but they often need to coexist on the same equipment.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation is intended to reduce the transmission of operational vibration from equipment into its support structure. Seismic restraint is intended to control earthquake-induced movement and transfer applicable forces through the support and structural attachment system.

A vibration isolator should therefore not automatically be treated as a seismic restraint.

Isolated Mechanical Equipment

Equipment such as chillers, air handling units, pumps, generators, cooling towers, and other mechanical systems may require vibration isolation during normal operation while also requiring seismic protection based on the project's applicable criteria.

The challenge is maintaining the required isolation characteristics without creating an unintended rigid path around the isolator.

Captive and Restrained Configurations

Captive vibration isolators can incorporate movement-control features that limit displacement under specified conditions. The configuration must be engineered so that normal operating vibration isolation and required restraint behavior work together.

Structural Attachment

Seismic forces ultimately have to travel through a defined load path. Depending on the application, that path can involve the equipment, mounting frame, isolator or restraint assembly, attachment plate, anchor bolts or structural connection, and supporting concrete or steel structure.

Codes and Project Criteria

Where seismic protection applies, engineers may need to consider ASCE 7, the IBC, CBC, HCAI requirements, Seismic Design Category, Risk Category, nonstructural component provisions, and project-specific structural criteria.

Healthcare projects can require particularly careful coordination because equipment anchorage, MEP systems, support assemblies, and documentation may be subject to project-specific HCAI requirements.

The exact requirements depend on the adopted code edition, jurisdiction, equipment classification, project specifications, and Engineer of Record criteria.

How Does BIM 3D CAD Improve Custom Isolator Design?

Custom isolation often involves tight dimensional relationships between equipment, mounts, support structures, MEP systems, and architectural clearances. BIM and 3D CAD can make these relationships easier to evaluate before fabrication.

Equipment and Structural Coordination

A three-dimensional model can represent equipment dimensions, isolator locations, mounting plates, structural framing, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, and maintenance clearances in the same spatial environment.

This is particularly useful for mechanical rooms, rooftops, industrial facilities, data centers, and healthcare projects where available space can be highly constrained.

Clash Detection

Potential conflicts can include an isolator occupying the same space as a pipe, a mounting bolt interfering with structural steel, insufficient access for installation, or a seismic restraint conflicting with a flexible connection.

Identifying these issues before fabrication can reduce field modifications and coordination problems.

Design Documentation

CAD models can support dimensional drawings, fabrication details, mounting-hole patterns, assembly drawings, and equipment interface documentation. This information helps connect engineering analysis to manufacturing.

Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

A practical workflow can follow:

Equipment Data → Engineering Analysis → 3D CAD → Detailed Design → Prototype → Fabrication → Verification

This is particularly valuable when custom vibration mounts must be manufactured in low quantities or when an OEM requires repeatable mounting geometry.

When Are Custom Equipment Mounts, Bases, and Frames Required?

A custom isolator does not always need to be a standalone component. In many projects, the most effective engineering solution involves a complete mounting assembly that connects the equipment, isolators, and supporting structure.

Equipment Mounting Frames

A fabricated equipment frame can provide a common interface when the equipment footprint does not align with available isolator locations. Structural steel, sheet metal, plates, brackets, and custom attachment points can be integrated into the assembly.

Inertia Bases

An inertia base can form part of a broader vibration-control strategy for selected mechanical equipment. Its mass and stiffness characteristics, equipment attachment, isolator arrangement, and structural interface should be evaluated as a complete system.

Mounting Plates and Brackets

Custom plates can accommodate nonstandard bolt patterns, while brackets can address height differences, side mounting, or restricted access. CNC machining may be useful where tight dimensional tolerances or specialized interfaces are required.

Retrofit Applications

Existing installations can create additional constraints. Replacement equipment may not match the dimensions or anchor pattern of the original unit. Existing structural members may restrict where supports can be installed, while obsolete isolation hardware may no longer be available.

Custom fabrication can bridge these differences by producing new mounting plates, brackets, rails, frames, or isolation assemblies around verified field conditions.

Integrated Fabrication

Laser cutting, plasma cutting, forming, welding, machining, and other fabrication processes can support the transition from engineering drawings to finished assemblies. The goal is to maintain the specified geometry and interfaces rather than treating fabrication as a separate activity from design.

How Are Custom Isolators Fabricated and Verified?

Engineering performance and physical fabrication are closely connected. A theoretically appropriate isolator can still create problems if its dimensions, mounting interfaces, material specifications, or assembly geometry are not accurately produced.

Cutting and Forming

Laser and plasma cutting can produce plates, brackets, housings, and other custom components. Forming operations can create sheet-metal geometries that would be difficult or inefficient to manufacture from a standard product.

Welding and Machining

Welded steel assemblies can provide structural mounting interfaces, while CNC machining can support precision holes, plates, spacers, adapters, and specialized equipment connections.

Welding also requires attention to distortion, dimensional control, joint configuration, and the material being processed.

Prototype and First Article Fabrication

A prototype can be useful when the equipment interface is unusual or when a design has not previously been manufactured. A first-article component can also provide an opportunity to verify fit, mounting geometry, clearances, and assembly procedures before a larger production run.

Dimensional Verification

Verification can include checking mounting-hole locations, overall dimensions, plate thickness, attachment interfaces, isolator positioning, and assembly tolerances.

For equipment supplied to multiple facilities or an OEM program, repeatability becomes particularly important. The same isolation characteristics and physical interfaces need to be reproduced consistently across production units.

How Should Engineers Evaluate a Custom Isolator Design?

A custom isolator should be evaluated against the complete application rather than a single specification such as load capacity.

Performance Requirements

Important parameters can include:

  • Static deflection

  • Natural frequency

  • Dynamic stiffness

  • Damping

  • Transmissibility

  • Operating frequency

  • Expected vibration amplitude

  • Movement limits

  • Shock requirements

  • Transient response

These parameters should be evaluated together because improving one characteristic can influence another.

Mechanical Requirements

The evaluation should also consider equipment weight, load distribution, center of gravity, mounting geometry, attachment hardware, support spacing, structural interfaces, and applicable load cases.

Environmental Requirements

Temperature, moisture, chemicals, corrosion, marine exposure, contamination, and outdoor conditions can influence both materials and expected service life.

Durability

Continuous industrial operation can produce millions of loading cycles. Fatigue, material degradation, wear, and cyclic deformation therefore deserve consideration when designing isolation components for long-term service.

Verification

Depending on the application, verification may include engineering calculations, manufacturer material data, prototype evaluation, dimensional inspection, vibration measurements, or application-specific testing.

For seismic applications, structural calculations and anchorage evaluation may also be required. Compliance should be demonstrated against the actual project criteria rather than inferred simply because a component is marketed as a vibration isolator.

What Information Is Needed to Start a Custom Isolator Design?

Good input data can significantly improve the efficiency and accuracy of the design process. The more clearly the equipment and installation conditions are defined, the less uncertainty exists between initial engineering and final fabrication.

Equipment Information

Useful information includes:

  • Equipment manufacturer and model

  • Operating weight

  • Overall dimensions

  • Center of gravity

  • Mounting points

  • Bolt-hole pattern

  • Equipment support surfaces

  • Operating speed

  • Manufacturer installation requirements

Dynamic Information

Where available, engineers should provide excitation frequencies, rotating or reciprocating characteristics, vibration measurements, startup and shutdown conditions, and known vibration problems.

If vibration data are unavailable, the equipment's operating characteristics can still provide an initial basis for analysis, followed by additional evaluation where required.

Structural Information

The design team may need information about the supporting slab, structural steel, equipment platform, existing anchors, available attachment points, and structural capacity.

For seismic applications, the project may also require seismic design criteria, component information, anchorage requirements, and EOR direction.

Environmental Information

Temperature, humidity, chemical exposure, marine conditions, outdoor installation, and corrosion risks can affect material and coating decisions.

Project Documentation

CAD files, BIM models, photographs, equipment drawings, existing isolation details, specifications, and field measurements can help establish the physical requirements.

Conclusion

Effective custom isolator design begins with the understanding that vibration isolation is a system-level engineering problem. The isolator must support the equipment mechanically while providing dynamic characteristics appropriate for the equipment's excitation, operating frequency, loading, geometry, environment, and required vibration performance.

That process starts with accurate equipment data. Weight and load distribution establish the basic support requirements, while center of gravity and mounting geometry influence stability and individual isolator loading. Operating speed and excitation frequency then provide the dynamic context needed to evaluate natural frequency, stiffness, damping, and transmissibility. Material selection adds another layer, particularly when the equipment operates in marine, industrial, outdoor, high-temperature, or corrosive environments.

In many applications, the isolator cannot be considered independently from the equipment mounting system. Custom plates, brackets, inertia bases, equipment frames, structural supports, and flexible connections may all be required to establish the correct physical interface. When the equipment is installed in a seismically regulated building, vibration isolation may also need to coexist with seismic restraint and anchorage designed around applicable project criteria.

BIM and 3D CAD further connect engineering intent with physical implementation by allowing equipment, isolation components, structural supports, MEP systems, and clearances to be coordinated before fabrication. Prototype and first-article manufacturing can provide another level of validation for specialized applications.

FAQ: Custom Isolator Design

What is custom isolator design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing a vibration isolation component or system around the requirements of specific equipment. It can account for equipment weight, load distribution, operating frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, dynamic stiffness, damping, mounting geometry, environmental exposure, and movement requirements.

Unlike selecting a standard mount solely from a load table, custom design considers how the isolator will behave within the complete equipment and support system.

When should a custom vibration isolator be used instead of a standard mount?

A custom solution may be appropriate when standard products cannot meet the required load, dimensions, stiffness, frequency response, mounting pattern, environmental resistance, movement limits, or structural interface.

This can occur with specialized industrial machinery, marine equipment, unusual HVAC installations, retrofit projects, OEM equipment, generators, machine tools, or applications with limited installation space.

What information is required for custom vibration isolator design?

Typical information includes equipment weight, dimensions, center of gravity, mounting points, operating speed, excitation frequencies, support arrangement, environmental conditions, and desired vibration performance.

Equipment drawings, manufacturer documentation, CAD models, photographs, existing isolation details, vibration measurements, and structural drawings can also help establish the design requirements.

What is the difference between spring, rubber, and wire rope isolators?

These isolation technologies have different mechanical characteristics. Spring isolators can provide significant vertical flexibility, while elastomeric isolators can be developed around specific stiffness and damping requirements. Wire rope isolators can provide multidirectional compliance and can be useful for applications involving vibration and shock.

The appropriate selection depends on equipment loading, excitation, movement, environmental exposure, fatigue requirements, and installation geometry. There is no single isolator type that is appropriate for every application.

Can custom isolators be designed for HVAC equipment?

Yes. Custom vibration isolation can be developed for air handling units, chillers, cooling towers, pumps, fans, compressors, boilers, generators, and other mechanical equipment.

The design may need to account for operating speed, equipment weight, support spacing, equipment base construction, flexible piping and duct connections, maintenance access, and seismic requirements where applicable.

Can a custom isolator also provide seismic restraint?

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint have different functions. The isolator is intended to reduce operational vibration transmission, while seismic restraint limits earthquake-induced movement and transfers applicable forces through the equipment support and structural attachment system.

Some applications can use captive or restrained isolation configurations, but the restraint must be engineered so it does not unintentionally create a rigid vibration path during normal equipment operation.

How does ASCE 7 affect custom isolator design?

ASCE 7 can become relevant when the isolation system is part of seismic protection for equipment or building systems. Depending on the project, engineers may need to consider seismic design category, component characteristics, seismic forces, anchorage, support conditions, and nonstructural component requirements.

The applicable provisions depend on the adopted code, project location, equipment classification, building characteristics, and project-specific engineering criteria. ASCE 7 should therefore not be treated as a universal design requirement for every vibration isolation application.

Are custom isolators used in healthcare facilities?

They can be used for healthcare HVAC systems, pumps, generators, mechanical equipment, laboratory equipment, and other applications where vibration control is important.

Healthcare projects may also involve additional seismic and documentation requirements. Where HCAI applies, equipment supports, anchorage, restraints, and related building-system components should be coordinated with the project's applicable HCAI requirements and Engineer of Record criteria.

Can custom isolators be fabricated for marine applications?

Yes. Marine equipment can create demanding requirements involving continuous vibration, shock, limited installation space, corrosion, moisture, and cyclic loading. Marine engine mounts and other custom isolation assemblies can therefore require careful material, geometry, stiffness, and fatigue considerations.

Stainless steel wire rope and corrosion-resistant metal components may be appropriate for selected marine environments, but material selection should be based on the actual exposure and mechanical requirements.

Does custom isolator design include custom mounting frames and brackets?

It can. A complete isolation system may require mounting plates, brackets, rails, equipment frames, inertia bases, structural supports, or custom steel assemblies.

These components can provide the interface between unusual equipment geometry and the selected isolators. They can also help coordinate equipment with existing structural steel, concrete supports, rooftop platforms, or retrofit conditions.

How does BIM 3D CAD support custom isolator design?

3D CAD and BIM can coordinate equipment dimensions, isolator locations, mounting interfaces, structural members, MEP systems, maintenance clearances, and seismic restraints.

This allows potential conflicts to be identified before fabrication and helps produce detailed manufacturing and installation information. For complex projects, the model can form an important connection between engineering analysis, fabrication, and field installation.
