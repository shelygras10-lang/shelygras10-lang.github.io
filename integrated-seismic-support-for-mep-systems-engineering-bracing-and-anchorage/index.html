Effective seismic protection depends on understanding how equipment and distribution systems are supported, how earthquake-induced forces move through each assembly, and how those forces ultimately transfer into the building structure. The complete path may include equipment, hangers, trapezes, strut channels, seismic braces, clamps, brackets, anchors, structural attachments, and the supporting slab, beam, wall, or frame.

HVAC equipment may require vibration isolation while also requiring seismic restraint. Piping may need to accommodate thermal movement while remaining adequately restrained during an earthquake. Electrical systems need support and restraint without creating conflicts with access, cable routing, or other MEP services. Rooftop equipment introduces additional coordination between equipment anchorage and the structural roof system.

The engineering process therefore begins with project-specific seismic criteria, component information, structural conditions, and MEP layouts. What Is Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems?

Integrated seismic support for MEP systems is a coordinated engineering approach that connects the supported component to the building structure through an intentional seismic load path. Instead of treating a brace, hanger, anchor, or support as an independent item, the complete assembly is considered from the MEP component through its structural attachment.

A typical load path can be represented as:

MEP Component → Support → Seismic Restraint → Brace Assembly → Connection → Anchorage → Structural Member

Each element has a different function. The MEP support carries gravity loads and establishes the physical position of the system. A seismic restraint limits earthquake-induced movement. The brace transfers applicable lateral or longitudinal forces into its connection. The connection transfers those forces to the anchor or structural attachment, and the building structure ultimately receives the resulting demand.

Ordinary Support vs. Seismic Support

A conventional hanger or support is generally designed around gravity loads and service requirements. Seismic support introduces another design condition: the system must be evaluated for earthquake-induced movement and applicable seismic forces.

This distinction matters because a support can be adequate for the weight of a pipe or duct while still being unsuitable for seismic demand. Threaded rods, clamps, beam attachments, anchors, and structural members all have capacities that must be considered as part of the complete assembly.

Why Integration Matters

Integrated design reduces the likelihood that a seismic brace will conflict with another MEP system or terminate at an attachment point that cannot safely accept the resulting force. It also provides a clearer basis for calculations, BIM coordination, fabrication drawings, installation, and inspection.

For large commercial and industrial projects, this approach helps structural and MEP disciplines work from the same assumptions. The result is not simply more hardware; it is a coordinated support strategy tied to the building's structural system.

How Are Seismic Support Requirements for MEP Systems Determined?

Seismic support requirements depend on project-specific conditions rather than a single universal bracing arrangement. Engineers first establish the seismic design criteria applicable to the building and then evaluate the characteristics of the nonstructural components that must be supported or restrained.

ASCE 7 provides the framework for seismic design of nonstructural components, including factors that can influence seismic demand. The adopted IBC or CBC establishes the broader building-code context, while project specifications and the Engineer of Record can introduce additional requirements.

Seismic Design Criteria

The seismic design category, building risk characteristics, component location, component properties, and other project parameters can influence the engineering approach. Components located at different elevations or attached to different portions of a building may experience different conditions.

The design process should therefore begin with the project's governing criteria instead of selecting a generic brace configuration based only on component size.

Component Characteristics

Weight, dimensions, support spacing, flexibility, center of mass, attachment configuration, and operating conditions can all influence seismic behavior. Large mechanical equipment may require dedicated anchorage or a fabricated mounting frame, while suspended distribution systems may use trapezes, strut channels, clamps, and directional restraints.

Seismic Load Path

Once seismic demand is established, the engineer must determine how the resulting forces move through the support system. A brace can only perform as intended if its connection, anchor, and structural attachment are capable of receiving the transferred load.

This is why integrated seismic support should not be reduced to selecting a product from a catalog. The engineering question is whether the entire assembly works as a connected system under the project's design conditions.

Engineering Documentation

Calculations, drawings, equipment information, structural plans, MEP layouts, manufacturer data, and field conditions may all be required. The more complex the building, the more important coordinated documentation becomes.

What Types of MEP Systems Require Integrated Seismic Support?

MEP systems encompass many components with different masses, geometries, materials, operating conditions, and support configurations. Integrated seismic support must therefore be adapted to the system rather than applied as one generic solution.

Mechanical Systems

Mechanical installations can include air handling units, chillers, pumps, boilers, cooling towers, generators, fans, compressors, and mechanical piping. Large equipment may require anchorage directly to a structural base, while suspended components may require engineered trapezes or restraint assemblies.

Mechanical rooms can be particularly challenging because equipment, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, and access pathways often occupy the same limited space.

Plumbing and Fire Protection

Plumbing and fire protection piping require coordinated support and seismic restraint. Pipe diameter, material, configuration, support spacing, movement requirements, and connection details influence the appropriate arrangement.

Seismic restraints must also be coordinated with valves, fittings, flexible connections, equipment connections, and other components that may have specific movement or accessibility requirements.

Electrical Systems

Conduit, cable trays, electrical distribution systems, and telecommunications infrastructure may require seismic support depending on the project conditions and applicable requirements. Their support systems must accommodate the routing geometry while maintaining access and coordination with other building services.

HVAC Ductwork

Large duct systems can require both transverse and longitudinal restraint. The restraint configuration must work with the duct support system and connect effectively to the structure.

Because ductwork often occupies large portions of ceiling space, seismic bracing must be coordinated carefully with lighting, ceilings, piping, cable trays, and structural framing.

How Does Seismic Bracing Work With MEP Supports and Hangers?

Seismic bracing becomes effective when it is integrated with the support system and structural attachment. MEP trapezes, strut channels, threaded rods, clamps, brackets, and brace members can form part of a coordinated assembly when their capacities and connections are appropriately engineered.

MEP Trapezes

A trapeze can support multiple pipes, conduits, or other MEP components from a shared structural connection. When designed for seismic conditions, the assembly may also incorporate restraint elements that control movement in specified directions.

The trapeze itself must be evaluated along with its rods, clamps, connections, and structural attachments. Increasing the size of one component does not automatically resolve a weakness elsewhere in the load path.

Strut Channel Systems

Strut channels are widely used for MEP support because they can accommodate configurable brackets, clamps, equipment supports, and attachment hardware. Custom strut channels can become particularly useful when standard dimensions do not work with structural geometry or MEP coordination requirements.

Transverse and Longitudinal Restraint

Seismic movement is not limited to one direction. Depending on the system and engineering requirements, restraints may be arranged to address movement perpendicular to or along the primary direction of a duct, pipe, tray, or other supported component.

The exact configuration and spacing must be established from the applicable design criteria and system characteristics rather than assumed from a generic rule.

Four-Way and Two-Way Restraint

Some applications require restraint in multiple directions, while others may require more limited directional control. The appropriate configuration depends on the component, support arrangement, expected movement, and project-specific seismic design.

How Is Seismic Anchorage Integrated With MEP Support Systems?

Seismic anchorage provides the connection between the support or restraint assembly and the building structure. It is therefore one of the most important parts of the integrated seismic load path.

A properly engineered brace can still fail to perform its intended function if the attachment point, anchor, or supporting structural member cannot accept the transferred demand.

Concrete Attachments

Concrete-supported MEP systems may use anchor bolts, mechanical anchors, adhesive anchors where appropriate, embedded components, attachment plates, or other engineered connections.

Anchor selection is only one part of the design. Concrete thickness, reinforcement, edge distance, embedment, spacing, substrate condition, and applicable failure modes can affect the capacity of the connection.

Structural Steel Attachments

Where structural steel is available, seismic supports may connect through beam clamps, bolted connections, welded assemblies, attachment plates, brackets, or other engineered details.

The connection must be compatible with the structural member and its intended load path. Attaching a brace to a convenient piece of steel does not automatically mean that the member is suitable for the resulting seismic demand.

Connection Capacity

The brace, connection, anchor, and supporting structure should be considered together. A high-capacity brace connected through an inadequate anchor remains an inadequate system.

This principle is especially important in retrofit projects, where existing structural conditions may differ from assumptions made during original construction.

Structural Load Path

The complete relationship is:

Component → Support → Brace → Connection → Anchor → Structure

Evaluating this sequence helps identify where additional reinforcement, custom brackets, structural analysis, or alternative attachment locations may be required.

How Are HVAC Systems Designed With Integrated Seismic Support?

HVAC systems combine large mechanical equipment with extensive ductwork, piping, electrical connections, and support structures. Their seismic design therefore requires coordination between equipment anchorage, suspended distribution systems, structural framing, and flexible interfaces.

Air handling units, chillers, pumps, cooling towers, boilers, and generators can impose substantial gravity and seismic demands on their supports. Equipment mounting frames and attachment plates may be needed where direct anchorage does not provide the required configuration.

HVAC Equipment

Equipment supports should account for equipment weight, support-point geometry, center of gravity, operating conditions, and seismic demand. Rooftop equipment adds another consideration: the roof structure must be capable of receiving the equipment and anchorage loads without compromising the intended load path.

Ductwork

Duct seismic restraint must be coordinated with hangers and surrounding construction. Brace locations can conflict with lighting, ceiling grids, piping, cable trays, or architectural elements if they are added late in the project.

BIM coordination can identify these conflicts before installation and allow brace locations to be adjusted while maintaining the required engineering intent.

Flexible Connections

HVAC equipment often uses flexible connections to accommodate vibration or relative movement. These connections must be considered together with seismic restraint so that the restraint system does not unintentionally create rigid load paths through components intended to remain flexible.

Rooftop Applications

Rooftop HVAC systems require coordination between equipment bases, structural steel or concrete supports, roof penetrations, waterproofing, anchorage, and maintenance access. Seismic support is therefore both a mechanical and structural coordination issue.

How Does Integrated Seismic Support Address Piping, Conduit, and Cable Trays?

Piping, conduit, and cable trays create long distribution networks that can extend across multiple structural bays and building zones. Their seismic support strategy must address movement along the system while maintaining functional connections and adequate structural attachment.

Seismic Pipe Supports

Pipe support systems can include clamps, trapezes, strut channels, threaded rods, brackets, and directional restraints. The arrangement must account for pipe geometry, support locations, connected equipment, valves, fittings, and flexible sections.

Piping may also experience thermal movement during normal operation. Seismic restraints should therefore be coordinated with the system's operational movement rather than installed in a way that creates unintended constraints.

Electrical Conduit

Conduit support must maintain the intended routing while allowing appropriate seismic restraint. Electrical installations are often coordinated through congested ceiling spaces, making brace orientation and attachment locations important to the overall construction sequence.

Cable Trays

Cable tray systems require support structures capable of carrying gravity loads and applicable seismic demands. Tray width, loading, support geometry, connections, and structural attachment all influence the engineering requirements.

Movement and Clearances

Seismic restraint is intended to control earthquake-induced movement, but surrounding components must still be considered. In a dense MEP installation, inadequate clearance can cause one system to impact another during movement.

The objective is therefore coordinated restraint rather than simply maximum rigidity.

How Should Seismic Support Be Coordinated With Vibration Isolation?

Seismic restraint and vibration isolation address different dynamic problems and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Vibration isolation is intended to reduce the transmission of dynamic forces and vibration between equipment and its supporting structure. Seismic restraint is intended to control earthquake-induced movement and transfer applicable seismic forces through an engineered load path.

Mechanical equipment can require both.

Isolated Mechanical Equipment

Spring isolators, rubber/metal isolators, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and captive vibration isolators can reduce vibration transmission from operating equipment.

However, an isolated piece of equipment can move more freely than rigidly anchored equipment. Its seismic restraint therefore has to be coordinated with the isolation system and the equipment's allowable movement.

Restrained Isolation Systems

Seismic restraint should not unintentionally short-circuit the intended vibration-isolation path. For example, an improperly arranged rigid connection can transfer vibration around an isolator and reduce the effectiveness of the isolation system.

Conversely, an isolation system that lacks appropriate seismic restraint may not provide the movement control required under earthquake conditions.

Flexible MEP Connections

Piping and duct connections to isolated equipment may require flexible components that accommodate equipment movement. These interfaces must be evaluated as part of the complete mechanical system.

The same principle applies to electrical connections and other services that cross between isolated equipment and fixed building systems.

Integrated design allows vibration performance, seismic restraint, equipment movement, and MEP flexibility to be considered simultaneously rather than as separate disciplines.

How Does BIM 3D CAD Improve Integrated MEP Seismic Support Design?

BIM and 3D CAD can transform seismic support coordination from a two-dimensional drawing exercise into a spatial engineering process. This is particularly useful when MEP systems are densely arranged or when structural attachment points are limited.

A coordinated model can represent structural members, slabs, walls, ducts, pipes, equipment, cable trays, conduit, supports, braces, and access zones. Engineers and contractors can then evaluate whether the proposed support arrangement physically fits before fabrication and installation.

MEP and Structural Coordination

Seismic braces frequently need to connect to structural elements that may already be occupied by other systems. BIM coordination can identify viable attachment zones and reveal where a brace would interfere with ductwork, piping, ceilings, lighting, or architectural components.

Clash Detection

Common conflicts include:

  • Seismic braces intersecting ductwork

  • Brace members conflicting with piping

  • Strut channels interfering with cable trays

  • Anchors landing near unsuitable structural locations

  • Equipment restraints blocking service access

  • Braces conflicting with ceiling systems

  • Structural attachments occupying congested framing zones

Resolving these conditions during design is generally more controlled than modifying assemblies in the field.

Fabrication and Installation

Once the support geometry is coordinated, the model can support fabrication drawings and custom components. Laser-cut plates, formed brackets, custom strut channels, mounting frames, and other metal assemblies can be developed from coordinated design information.

A practical workflow is:

Project Data → Seismic Analysis → BIM/CAD Coordination → Support Design → Engineering Review → Fabrication → Installation → Field Verification

What Materials and Components Are Used in Integrated Seismic MEP Support?

Material selection depends on structural demand, environment, fabrication requirements, corrosion exposure, temperature, and project specifications. Steel is common because of its structural properties and broad fabrication options, but different steel types and protective finishes may be appropriate for different applications.

Carbon steel and structural steel can be used for many support frames, brackets, plates, strut components, and brace assemblies. Stainless steel may be appropriate where corrosion resistance is important, particularly in demanding environments. High-strength and alloy steels may be considered when specific mechanical properties are required.

Galvanized steel and powder-coated components can provide corrosion protection where their characteristics are compatible with the application. The finish should be selected according to environmental exposure and service conditions rather than treated as a universal requirement.

Elastomeric materials also appear where seismic support interfaces with vibration isolation. Rubber, neoprene, isolation pads, and related compounds can be part of equipment mounting systems, although their function differs from that of structural steel seismic braces.

Custom fabrication can combine these materials into application-specific assemblies. For example, a project may require a formed stainless-steel bracket, a structural steel support frame, a custom strut assembly, and appropriate anchorage hardware. The important engineering consideration is how each component contributes to the complete support and load-transfer system.

What Are the Seismic Support Requirements for Hospitals and Critical Facilities?

Hospitals and other critical facilities require particularly careful coordination because MEP systems may support essential building functions. HVAC, electrical distribution, emergency power, plumbing, fire protection, and mechanical systems can all be important to facility operation following an earthquake.

Healthcare projects can also involve requirements administered through the California Department of Health Care Access and Information, formerly associated with OSHPD. HCAI projects may require specific documentation, review processes, pre-approval considerations, and engineering coordination beyond what is encountered on a conventional commercial project.

Critical MEP Systems

Hospital support systems can include emergency generators, air handling equipment, medical-support infrastructure, electrical distribution, piping, fire protection, and other systems. Equipment anchorage and suspended MEP restraint must be coordinated with structural conditions and facility requirements.

Documentation

Calculations, shop drawings, equipment data, anchorage details, structural attachment information, and installation documentation can all become important parts of the project record.

Field Verification

Existing conditions are particularly important during healthcare renovations. Structural members, slab thickness, existing anchors, ceiling conditions, and MEP routing may differ from original drawings.

A coordinated engineering process can identify these differences before fabrication. When Are Custom MEP Seismic Support Systems Required?

Standard seismic components can be useful when their dimensions, capacities, connections, and installation requirements align with the project. Custom MEP seismic support systems become more relevant when standard components cannot efficiently accommodate the structural or MEP conditions.

Congested mechanical rooms are a common example. A brace may need to pass around existing piping while connecting to a specific structural member. A standard configuration may not provide the necessary geometry, requiring a custom bracket, attachment plate, trapeze, or strut assembly.

Custom Strut Channels

Custom strut channels can address unusual support dimensions, equipment layouts, or attachment requirements. They may also simplify fabrication when several individual standard components would otherwise be required.

Equipment-Specific Supports

Large mechanical equipment may require custom mounting frames or plates that distribute loads to designated structural attachment points. These assemblies can be designed around equipment dimensions and field constraints.

Retrofit Applications

Retrofit projects frequently have limited structural attachment locations. Existing beams, slabs, walls, and framing may already support other systems, making coordination essential.

How Are Integrated Seismic Support Systems Evaluated for Compliance?

Compliance is not established simply because an individual component is described as seismic-rated. The complete support system must be evaluated against the applicable project criteria, including the component, brace, connection, anchorage, and supporting structure.

ASCE 7 provides important seismic design provisions, including requirements addressing nonstructural components. The IBC and CBC provide building-code frameworks, while HCAI requirements may apply to qualifying healthcare facilities in California.

Engineering Calculations

Calculations may evaluate seismic demand, support reactions, brace forces, connection capacity, anchor capacity, and structural attachment conditions. The specific calculations required depend on the project and governing criteria.

Structural Coordination

The building structure must be capable of accepting the forces transferred from MEP supports. This can require coordination with the structural engineer to determine whether existing beams, slabs, walls, or other members are suitable.

Component Verification

Equipment and MEP system information should be checked against the design assumptions. Weight, dimensions, support points, manufacturer requirements, and connection details can all affect the final arrangement.

Installation Verification

A properly engineered support can still perform incorrectly if it is installed in a different location, attached to an unsuitable structural element, oriented incorrectly, or modified without engineering review.

Field verification should therefore confirm the installed condition against approved drawings and calculations. This closes the engineering loop from design through construction.

Conclusion: Designing Integrated MEP Seismic Support as a Complete Load Path

Integrated seismic support for MEP systems is fundamentally a coordination problem as much as it is a hardware problem. Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems occupy the same building environment, but each system has different characteristics, support requirements, movement behavior, and structural interfaces. Effective seismic design connects these systems to the building structure through deliberate and verifiable load paths.

The essential relationship remains:

MEP Component → Support → Seismic Restraint → Brace → Connection → Anchorage → Structural Member

Evaluating the entire path helps prevent common weaknesses such as inadequate structural attachments, unsuitable anchors, improperly oriented braces, support conflicts, and disconnected assumptions between MEP and structural drawings. It also makes it easier to coordinate seismic restraint with normal operational requirements such as thermal movement, equipment maintenance, vibration isolation, and flexible connections.

For HVAC systems, the challenge may involve coordinating equipment anchorage, duct bracing, piping, and vibration isolation. For electrical systems, conduit and cable trays must be restrained without disrupting routing and access. For piping, seismic restraint must be balanced against thermal and operational movement. In hospitals, laboratories, data centers, and other critical facilities, these considerations can become even more significant because the continued operation of MEP infrastructure may be an important project objective.

BIM 3D CAD provides another layer of control by allowing engineers and contractors to coordinate braces, supports, structural members, equipment, and access zones before fabrication. Where standard assemblies cannot accommodate actual conditions, custom strut channels, brackets, mounting frames, attachment plates, and fabricated steel assemblies can provide project-specific solutions.

From there, the support system can be engineered as a complete structural load path rather than as a collection of independent braces and hardware.

Frequently Asked Questions About Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems

What is integrated seismic support for MEP systems?

Integrated seismic support is a coordinated approach to protecting mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems against earthquake-induced movement. It considers the MEP component, support or hanger, seismic restraint, brace, connection, anchorage, and building structure as one load-transfer system.

This approach is different from installing individual braces without evaluating how forces travel through the complete assembly. The objective is to establish an intentional path from the supported component into the building structure while coordinating the system with surrounding MEP services and normal operating movement.

When is seismic bracing required for MEP systems?

Requirements depend on the project's governing code, seismic design criteria, component characteristics, building conditions, and applicable project specifications. ASCE 7 includes provisions addressing seismic design of nonstructural components, while the adopted IBC or CBC establishes the applicable building-code framework.

The exact requirements should be determined from project-specific engineering criteria rather than a generic assumption based only on pipe size, duct size, or equipment weight.

What is the difference between MEP seismic support and ordinary MEP support?

Ordinary MEP supports primarily position components and carry gravity loads. Seismic support adds an engineered mechanism for controlling earthquake-induced movement and transferring applicable seismic forces into the building structure.

A gravity hanger may support the weight of a pipe or duct but may not be adequate to resist the forces associated with seismic movement. Seismic support can involve braces, strut channels, clamps, brackets, anchors, and structural attachments designed as part of a complete load path.

How does ASCE 7 affect seismic support for MEP systems?

ASCE 7 provides seismic design provisions that can influence the evaluation of nonstructural components and their supports. Project-specific parameters such as seismic design category and component characteristics can affect the resulting design requirements.

ASCE 7 should be considered together with the adopted building code, project specifications, structural criteria, manufacturer requirements, and EOR direction. A generic brace configuration should not be treated as universally compliant without evaluating the actual project conditions.

Do HVAC equipment and ductwork require seismic restraint?

Depending on the project conditions and applicable requirements, HVAC equipment and ductwork may require seismic restraint. Large equipment can require dedicated anchorage or mounting assemblies, while suspended ductwork may require directional seismic restraint.

HVAC systems also frequently include vibration isolation. Spring isolators, rubber/metal isolators, captive isolators, or other isolation systems may need to be coordinated with seismic restraint so that earthquake protection does not unintentionally compromise the intended vibration-isolation function.

How are pipes, conduits, and cable trays protected against earthquake movement?

Pipes, conduits, and cable trays can use engineered support and restraint assemblies incorporating trapezes, strut channels, clamps, threaded rods, brackets, brace members, and structural attachments.

The specific arrangement depends on the system geometry, support configuration, seismic criteria, and structural conditions. Both transverse and longitudinal movement may need consideration, and restraint must be coordinated with surrounding systems and operational movement.

Can vibration isolation systems be combined with seismic restraints?

Yes, mechanical equipment can require both vibration isolation and seismic restraint because the two systems address different dynamic conditions. Vibration isolation reduces transmission of operating vibration, while seismic restraint controls earthquake-induced movement.

The two systems must be coordinated carefully. An improperly installed rigid restraint can create an unintended vibration path around an isolator, while an inadequately restrained isolated system may experience excessive seismic movement.

How are MEP seismic supports attached to concrete and structural steel?

Concrete attachments can use engineered anchors, attachment plates, embedded components, or other appropriate connection methods. Structural steel attachments may use beam clamps, bolted connections, welded assemblies, brackets, or fabricated attachment plates.

The connection must be evaluated as part of the complete load path. Anchor capacity alone does not establish system adequacy if the brace, connection, or supporting structural member cannot safely receive the transferred demand.

What role does BIM play in integrated seismic support design?

BIM and 3D CAD help coordinate MEP supports and seismic restraints with structural members, equipment, ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, ceilings, and access areas.

This can reveal clashes before fabrication and installation. It can also support detailed fabrication drawings for custom brackets, strut assemblies, mounting frames, and other components. For complex projects, BIM provides a practical connection between engineering analysis, spatial coordination, fabrication, and construction.

Are hospitals subject to additional seismic support requirements?

Healthcare facilities can be subject to additional requirements depending on jurisdiction and project type. In California, qualifying healthcare projects may fall under HCAI requirements and associated review and documentation processes.

Hospital MEP systems may include critical HVAC, emergency power, plumbing, fire protection, and electrical infrastructure. Their supports and restraints therefore require careful coordination between structural engineering, MEP design, equipment manufacturers, contractors, and project authorities.

When should custom MEP seismic support systems or custom strut channels be used?

Custom systems become useful when standard components cannot accommodate structural geometry, equipment dimensions, limited installation space, congested MEP layouts, retrofit conditions, or specialized attachment requirements.

Custom solutions may include strut channels, trapezes, mounting frames, brackets, gusset plates, attachment plates, and fabricated steel assemblies. The objective should be to solve a specific engineering or coordination requirement while maintaining a documented load path and appropriate structural attachment.

What information is needed for MEP seismic support calculations and engineering?

Useful information includes the project's seismic design criteria, MEP equipment schedules, component weights and dimensions, support locations, structural drawings, equipment manufacturer information, MEP layouts, existing-condition documentation, and proposed attachment points.

For complex systems, BIM models, CAD files, photographs, field measurements, and fabrication requirements can also be valuable. Providing this information early allows the seismic support system to be evaluated as an integrated assembly instead of designing individual components without knowing their structural and MEP interfaces.
